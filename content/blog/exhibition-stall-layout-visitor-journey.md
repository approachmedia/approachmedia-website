# Exhibition Stall Layout Design: How to Plan a Stall That Generates Leads

Some exhibition stalls look stunning in photos but bring in very few leads. Others look simpler but keep the sales team busy for three days straight.

The difference is usually the layout.

In 2026, leading stall designers are moving away from "aesthetically balanced" layouts towards **visitor-journey zoning**: planning the stall around how people actually approach, enter and move through the space.

Here's the approach we use at Approach Media after building more than 6,000 stalls.

## The 8-second test

A visitor walking down an aisle decides very quickly whether to stop. Many designers work with a simple test: **can someone understand what you do within about eight seconds?**

If the answer is no, the rest of the stall doesn't matter, because visitors won't step inside.

## The 4-zone visitor journey

### Zone 1: Attract

This is what visitors see from the aisle, often from 30 feet or more away.

- A bold, readable headline, ideally five words or fewer, that says what you offer
- Your logo placed high where it's visible over crowds
- One strong visual or hero product
- Good lighting to stand out in the hall

### Zone 2: Orient

As visitors come closer, they need to understand quickly what you do and whether it's relevant to them.

- Clear product or solution categories
- Simple signage, not long paragraphs
- Open entry points with no barriers

### Zone 3: Engage

This is where interest turns into interaction.

- Product displays and live demos
- Touchscreens or short interactive experiences
- Staff positioned to start conversations naturally

### Zone 4: Convert

This is where leads are captured and business discussions happen.

- A meeting counter or discussion area
- Comfortable seating for serious conversations
- Lead capture positioned where conversations naturally end
- A semi-private space for key clients

## Open layouts perform better

Crowded, closed-off stalls create psychological barriers. Industry data suggests minimalist, open layouts can create 30 to 40% more usable floor space and make visitors more comfortable stepping in.

Tips for an open layout:

- Avoid blocking the front with a long counter
- Keep walkways clear and wide enough for groups
- Put tall elements at the back or sides, not the front
- Leave space for people to pause without blocking others

## Layout by stall type

**Inline stall (one side open):** keep the back wall strong, place the headline high and keep the front fully open.

**Corner stall (two sides open):** use both aisles and place the hero element at the corner for visibility from two directions.

**Peninsula stall (three sides open):** create a strong central feature and plan flows from three directions.

**Island stall (four sides open):** design as a landmark, with a central tower or hanging structure, and meeting zones inside.

## Common layout mistakes

- Filling every square foot with products
- Placing the reception counter as a barrier at the entrance
- Hiding meeting spaces where nobody can find them
- Using small text that can't be read from the aisle
- Forgetting storage, which ends up cluttering the stall
- Big screens placed where they block movement

## Don't forget practical requirements

A great layout still needs to work within:

- Venue height and structure rules
- Electrical load and power points
- Fire safety and exit guidelines
- Build-up and dismantling timelines

This is why we plan layouts with feasibility in mind from day one.

## LinkedIn Post

Most exhibition stalls are designed to look good in photos.

The ones that actually generate leads are designed differently.

After 6,000+ stalls, here's the framework we use: **the 4-zone visitor journey.**

1️⃣ **Attract** – a headline of 5 words or fewer, readable from 30 feet
2️⃣ **Orient** – visitors understand what you do in about 8 seconds
3️⃣ **Engage** – demos and interactions where staff can start conversations
4️⃣ **Convert** – a meeting space and lead capture where talks naturally end

And one simple rule: **don't block the entrance.**

A long counter at the front feels like a wall. Open layouts invite people in.

Try this at your next show: stand in the aisle, look at your stall for 8 seconds, and ask, "Would I know what this company does?"

If not, the layout needs work.

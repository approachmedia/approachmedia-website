# Stand Design Approval: How Organizer Submissions Actually Work

Somewhere between "we love this design" and "the stall is being built" sits a step most first-time exhibitors discover too late: **the organizer has to approve your stand before anyone builds it.** Miss the process and the best design in the hall stays a rendering.

This guide explains the approval machinery — what gets submitted, when, what goes wrong, and whose job it should be. It applies to every custom stall at every organized show; the specifics for *your* show live in one document we'll keep pointing at.

## Why approvals exist

Organizers answer to venues, insurers and safety authorities for everything built on their floor. Approval is how they verify your stall won't block aisles or sightlines, exceed height limits, overload floors or power, violate fire-safety norms, or interfere with neighbouring stands. It's not bureaucracy for its own sake — it's the system that lets hundreds of independent construction projects happen in one hall in three days.

## The document that governs everything: the exhibitor manual

Every organized show publishes an exhibitor manual (at US shows, an "exhibitor kit"). It defines your show's height limits by stall type and position, what must be submitted for approval and by when, service order forms (power, water, rigging, internet) and their deadlines, build and dismantle windows, and the rules for special structures. We say this in every guide we write because it's the single most consequential habit in exhibiting: **the day the manual arrives, it goes to your stall builder.** Unread manuals are how good designs get rejected in week minus-two.

## What a typical submission contains

Exact requirements vary by show — the manual is definitive — but a custom-stall submission generally includes:

- **Stall drawings** — plans and elevations with dimensions, showing the design as it will actually be built (concept renders are not drawings)
- **Height and layout compliance** — how the design sits within your stall type's limits: open sides, setbacks, sightline rules where they apply
- **Materials and fire-safety declarations** — what the stall is built from, per the show's safety norms
- **Electrical load declaration** — total power requirement, driving both approval and your service order
- **Rigging/suspension requests** — anything hung from the venue ceiling is almost always a separate, earlier approval
- **Extra engineering for special structures** — double-deck and mezzanine stalls add structural drawings, load calculations and engineer certification, on earlier deadlines; [we've covered that process in detail](/blog/double-decker-stall-rules-india)

## When the deadlines really fall

The pattern that surprises exhibitors: **approval deadlines land weeks before the show — often before you expected to be finished designing.** Rigging and double-deck approvals close earliest; standard stall drawings typically follow; service orders often carry early-bird pricing that quietly expires. This is a big part of why our standard guidance is to book your builder [six to eight weeks out](/blog/exhibition-stall-design-cost-india) — not because fabrication takes that long, but because design-freeze must happen early enough for submission, correction and resubmission if the organizer asks for changes.

## What gets designs rejected

The recurring causes are unremarkable and avoidable: heights over the limit for the stall's position, closed walls where open sides are required, missing structural details for anything unusual, late submission, and drawings that don't match what's actually being built (which surfaces at inspection — the worst possible moment). An experienced builder designs *within* the manual from day one, so approval is a formality rather than a negotiation.

## Whose job is this?

Formally, the exhibitor's. Practically, it should be your stall builder's — they produce the drawings, they know the norms, and they carry the consequences of a rejection. This is core to what [turnkey project management](/services/turnkey-project-management) means at Approach Media: manual review, drawing production, submissions, service orders and deadline tracking handled by the team building the stall — one team, one invoice, and no discovery in week minus-two. ## FAQ

**Does every exhibition stall need organizer approval?**
Custom-built stalls at organized trade shows generally do; shell-scheme stalls (pre-built by the organizer) usually need approval only for modifications. Your show's exhibitor manual is definitive.

**When are stand drawings due?**
It varies by show, but typically weeks before opening — with rigging and double-deck approvals due earliest. Check the manual on day one and put every deadline in a calendar.

**What happens if a design is submitted late or rejected?**
Outcomes range from forced design changes to refusal of special structures. Late double-deck submissions are commonly refused outright — timelines exist in the manual precisely so this doesn't happen.

**Who submits the drawings — the exhibitor or the stall builder?**
Formally the exhibitor; practically, a capable builder handles the whole process as part of their scope. Ask any builder you're evaluating exactly who owns submissions.

**Do international shows work the same way?**
The same logic with local variations — European venues run structured contractor approvals, US shows work through the exhibitor kit with venue labour rules.
